Dynamical isometry: why variance preservation is insufficient

From average gradient size to the full Jacobian spectrum: Gaussian and orthogonal weights, nonlinear gates, residual products, and the directions that a network preserves or loses.

A network can preserve the average size of a gradient while almost erasing some directions and greatly amplifying others. Dynamical isometry asks for a stronger form of propagation: the relevant singular values of the input-output Jacobian should remain close to one. This note starts with matrices whose behavior is visible by inspection, then explains why random weights, nonlinear gates, and residual products behave differently.

This expands §7 of the DIP note. Unless stated otherwise, layers have the same width \(n\), the Jacobian is with respect to activations, and the analysis concerns initialization. A parameter Jacobian and a weight-update matrix are different objects, as discussed in the NTK and spectral-scaling notes.

1. The Jacobian tells us which perturbations survive

For a map \(h_L=F(h_0)\), let

\[ J=\frac{\partial h_L}{\partial h_0}. \]

A small forward perturbation obeys \(\delta h_L=J\,\delta h_0\). A backward loss gradient obeys \(g_0=J^\top g_L\). Take a singular-value decomposition

\[ J=U\,\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n)V^\top. \]

The input direction \(v_i\) becomes \(s_i u_i\). The output-gradient direction \(u_i\) becomes \(s_i v_i\) when propagated backward. The same singular values govern both kinds of sensitivity.

A value near zero means a local direction is almost lost. A large value means a small perturbation can have a large effect. If every \(s_i=1\), the map preserves the Euclidean length of every infinitesimal perturbation, although it may rotate those perturbations.

Local property. A Jacobian describes sensitivity around one input and one parameter state. It does not by itself imply that the network preserves all finite distances, is globally invertible, or generalizes well.

2. What average gradient preservation actually controls

Let \(v\) be a uniformly random unit vector, independent of a fixed square Jacobian \(J\). Since \(\mathbb E[vv^\top]=I/n\),

\[ \mathbb E_v\|Jv\|^2 =\frac1n\operatorname{tr}(J^\top J) =\frac1n\sum_i s_i^2. \]

Define \(m_1=n^{-1}\sum_i s_i^2\). Setting \(m_1=1\) preserves squared length averaged over isotropic test directions. It says nothing about how unevenly that sensitivity is distributed.

The backward statement is identical for an independent isotropic output gradient. An actual loss gradient need not be isotropic or independent of the weights. This is another reason to distinguish a diagnostic average from a guarantee about a particular task.

The mean-field criticality factor \(\chi=\sigma_w^2\mathbb E[\phi'(Z)^2]\) is designed to control this sort of average under the relevant initialization assumptions. It is one spectral moment, not the entire spectrum.

3. A critical matrix can erase half the space

For even \(n\), compare

\[ J_{\mathrm{good}}=I,\qquad J_{\mathrm{bad}}=\operatorname{diag} (\underbrace{\sqrt2,\ldots,\sqrt2}_{n/2}, \underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{n/2}). \]

Both have \(m_1=1\). The second erases every perturbation in half the coordinate directions and expands the other half. Average gradient preservation is fully compatible with exact information loss.

A full-rank version is

\[ J_\varepsilon= \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{2-\varepsilon^2},\varepsilon), \qquad 0<\varepsilon<1. \]

Again \(m_1=1\), but its condition number is \(\sqrt{2-\varepsilon^2}/\varepsilon\), which can be arbitrarily large. Avoiding exact zeros is therefore not enough.

This example is useful whenever a variance calculation seems to settle a trainability question: preserving the sum of squared sensitivities does not ensure that the task-relevant directions retain useful sensitivity.

4. A precise local version of dynamical isometry

A strong quantitative criterion is

\[ \|J^\top J-I\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\delta,\qquad 0\leq\delta<1. \]

Equivalently, for every input perturbation \(v\),

\[ (1-\delta)\|v\|^2\leq\|Jv\|^2 \leq(1+\delta)\|v\|^2. \]

Every singular value then lies between \(\sqrt{1-\delta}\) and \(\sqrt{1+\delta}\). The worst-case condition number is bounded by \(\sqrt{(1+\delta)/(1-\delta)}\).

Random-matrix papers also discuss concentration of the empirical distribution of singular values near one. That is related but weaker than a uniform operator-norm statement: a small fraction of outliers can escape an empirical-distribution limit.

Pennington, Schoenholz, and Ganguli (2017) study the full initialization spectrum in nonlinear networks. The practical lesson is to inspect spectral spread and extremes alongside the mean.

5. Deep linear networks give an exact benchmark

Take \(h_\ell=W_\ell h_{\ell-1}\), with square matrices. Then

\[ J=W_LW_{L-1}\cdots W_1. \]

If each \(W_\ell=O_\ell\) is orthogonal, the product is orthogonal:

\[ J^\top J =O_1^\top\cdots O_L^\top O_L\cdots O_1=I. \]

This is exact at every width and every depth. Independent Haar-distributed orthogonal matrices have entry second moment \(1/n\), matching the variance of an iid Gaussian initializer with entries \(\mathcal N(0,1/n)\). Matching entry variance therefore does not match the Jacobian spectrum.

If \(W_\ell=\sigma_\ell O_\ell\), all singular values of \(J\) equal \(\prod_\ell|\sigma_\ell|\). The overall gain still needs control; orthogonality controls anisotropy, not an arbitrary scalar multiplier.

Deep linear models are linear in inputs but nonlinear in their layer factors. Their usefulness for studying initialization and learning dynamics is developed by Saxe, McClelland, and Ganguli.

6. One Gaussian layer is already spectrally broad

Let \(W\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\) have independent entries with variance \(1/n\). For a fixed input \(x\),

\[ \mathbb E_W\|Wx\|^2=\|x\|^2. \]

At large \(n\), the squared singular values have the square Marchenko–Pastur density

\[ p(\lambda)=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{4-\lambda}{\lambda}}, \qquad 0<\lambda<4. \]

This distribution has mean one and variance one. Its support reaches zero, and its upper edge is four. The largest singular value approaches two, rather than one, while the smallest approaches zero.

A finite Gaussian matrix is full rank almost surely. The limiting support touching zero means increasingly weak directions, not a positive fraction of exactly zero singular values. This differs from the exact rank loss caused by a ReLU gate.

The random-matrix calculation and its deep-product extension are used in Pennington et al.'s linear-network analysis. The contrast with an orthogonal matrix is already visible after one layer.

7. Gaussian products preserve the first moment and spread the second

There is a short moment calculation that exposes the depth problem. Let \(A=J_\ell J_\ell^\top\), and append an independent Gaussian layer \(W\) with entry variance \(1/n\). The new squared-Jacobian matrix is \(B=WAW^\top\).

Conditioning on \(A\), the Gaussian second- and fourth-moment identities give

\[ \mathbb E_W\!\left[\frac{\operatorname{tr}B}{n}\right] =\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{n}, \] \[ \mathbb E_W\!\left[\frac{\operatorname{tr}B^2}{n}\right] =\left(1+\frac1n\right)\frac{\operatorname{tr}A^2}{n} +\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{n}\right)^2. \]

One way to see the second formula is to separate equal and unequal rows of \(W\). For distinct rows \(w_i,w_j\), \(\mathbb E(w_i^\top Aw_j)^2=\operatorname{tr}(A^2)/n^2\); for one row, \(\mathbb E(w_i^\top Aw_i)^2=[(\operatorname{tr}A)^2+2\operatorname{tr}(A^2)]/n^2\).

Taking width to infinity at fixed depth, the normalized moments concentrate. Starting from \(J_0=I\), this gives

\[ m_1(L)=1,\qquad m_2(L)=L+1,\qquad \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(s^2)=m_2-m_1^2=L.} \]

Every layer is variance preserving, but the squared singular values become more dispersed with depth. The first moment misses the accumulated anisotropy.

9. A nonlinearity inserts an input-dependent gate

For \(z_\ell=W_\ell h_{\ell-1}+b_\ell\) and \(h_\ell=\phi(z_\ell)\), define

\[ D_\ell=\operatorname{diag}(\phi'(z_{\ell,1}),\ldots,\phi'(z_{\ell,n})). \]

The Jacobian becomes

\[ \boxed{J=D_LW_L\cdots D_1W_1.} \]

Orthogonal weights can preserve length before each gate, but the diagonal gate stretches different coordinates by different amounts. If those diagonal entries are all close to the same positive number, a scalar gain can approximately compensate. If many entries are zero or strongly unequal, it cannot.

At a stationary preactivation distribution, the usual mean-field gain is

\[ \chi=\sigma_w^2\mathbb E[\phi'(Z)^2]. \]

The condition \(\chi=1\) controls an average squared gain per layer. It does not determine the distribution of \(\phi'(Z)^2\), or remove the spectral spread inherited from the weight matrices.

10. Why orthogonal ReLU weights still lose directions

For a centered continuous symmetric preactivation distribution, a ReLU derivative is zero on about half the coordinates and one on the rest. With \(W=\sqrt2\,O\),

\[ A=DW,\qquad AA^\top=2D. \]

A single square layer therefore has squared singular values approximately half zero and half two. Their mean is one, but the layer is not isometric. This reproduces the counterexample in §3 inside an actual nonlinear layer.

For a product, rank cannot exceed the rank of any factor. One such gate already prevents full-space dynamical isometry in this equal-width architecture.

Do not multiply active fractions into a rank formula. A depth-\(L\) ReLU stack does not generally retain only a fraction \(2^{-L}\) of dimensions. Dense matrices mix the surviving subspaces between gates. The rank bound from a gate is valid; a product-of-fractions argument is not.

This conclusion concerns square feed-forward Jacobians on the full activation space. A wide layer mapping a lower-dimensional input, a residual identity path, or a restriction to a data manifold can change the relevant rank and isometry question.

11. Smooth activations can approach the linear benchmark

For tanh,

\[ \phi'(z)=\operatorname{sech}^2z=1-z^2+O(z^4). \]

If preactivations remain near zero, most derivative gates are close to one. Orthogonal weights then remove one source of spectral spread, and the gates introduce only a small deviation from the linear orthogonal case.

But “near zero” must be maintained by the forward variance recursion. At large depth, small per-layer deviations can still accumulate. Choosing a gain to set \(\chi=1\) fixes the mean squared gain, while the variation in the derivative gates determines how close the full spectrum can remain to one.

This explains why the activation and weight ensemble have to be designed together. The nonlinear isometry analysis of Pennington et al. makes this dependence explicit.

There is also a tradeoff to interpret carefully: operating near a linear part of the activation can help propagation at initialization. It does not imply that the model must remain a linear function of inputs throughout training, nor prove that the most nearly isometric initialization gives the best final predictor.

12. What the random-matrix machinery adds

Moment calculations like §7 become harder when gates and weights are multiplied repeatedly. Random-matrix methods organize the spectrum of these products instead of tracking every entry.

A useful mental model is that covariance recursion tracks how large a typical signal becomes, while spectral analysis tracks the distribution of gains over all local directions. Free-probability transforms can turn suitable large-matrix product calculations into algebraic relations between spectral distributions.

The assumptions are essential. Independence of entries is not the same as the asymptotic freeness used by the spectral calculation. In a nonlinear network, \(D_\ell\) depends on preactivations produced using \(W_\ell\), and trained weights acquire further correlations.

The exact orthogonal linear argument and the conditional Gaussian moment calculation in this note do not need a heuristic independence assumption between nonlinear gates and weights. Extending them to nonlinear architectures requires the additional arguments in the source theory; extending initialization results to trained networks requires still more.

13. Residual connections replace gates by perturbations of identity

For \(h_{\ell+1}=h_\ell+a_\ell F_\ell(h_\ell)\), let \(B_\ell=DF_\ell(h_\ell)\). Then

\[ J=\prod_{\ell=L-1}^{0}(I+a_\ell B_\ell). \]

A ReLU inside \(F_\ell\) can kill branch sensitivities without directly killing the identity route. This is a major structural change from \(D_\ell W_\ell\).

If \(|a_\ell|\|B_\ell\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\varepsilon_\ell<1\), elementary singular-value bounds give

\[ 1-\varepsilon_\ell\leq s_i(I+a_\ell B_\ell)\leq1+\varepsilon_\ell, \] \[ \prod_\ell(1-\varepsilon_\ell) \leq s_{\min}(J)\leq s_{\max}(J) \leq\prod_\ell(1+\varepsilon_\ell). \]

A bounded sum \(\sum_\ell\varepsilon_\ell\), with individual terms bounded away from one, gives coarse control of both extremes. To force this worst-case bound close to one, that sum must be small.

Random centered branches can exhibit cancellations that make a sum of squared multipliers relevant instead. That stochastic calculation is more favorable than the worst-case bound, but requires assumptions on the matrices and their dependencies. The two arguments should not be interchanged.

14. A smooth depth limit need not be isometric

Take the deterministic two-dimensional branch Jacobian \(B=\operatorname{diag}(b,-b)\), with the same \(b>0\) at every depth, and use \(a=1/L\). Then

\[ J_L=(I+B/L)^L \longrightarrow e^B=\operatorname{diag}(e^b,e^{-b}). \]

The depth limit exists and both singular values remain finite and nonzero. Yet the condition number tends to \(e^{2b}\). For \(b=1\), the gains are approximately \(2.72\) and \(0.37\), giving a condition number of about \(7.39\).

This is a stable, smooth evolution through depth with appreciable distortion. Bounded propagation and dynamical isometry are distinct requirements.

For a continuous flow \(J'(t)=B(t)J(t)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dt}(J^\top J)=J^\top(B^\top+B)J. \]

If \(B(t)\) is skew-symmetric, the metric \(J^\top J\) remains constant. Its symmetric part is what stretches and contracts directions. A \(1/L\) residual multiplier alone imposes no such skew-symmetry constraint.

15. Activation conditioning and parameter learning are different

There are at least three matrices that might be called “the Jacobian” in these notes:

MatrixPerturbation being measuredRelevant question
\(D_{h_0}h_L\)Input or hidden-state perturbationsCan signals and sensitivities propagate?
\(D_\theta\mathbf f\)Parameter perturbations on a datasetWhich prediction changes can training produce?
\(D_{\theta_\ell}F_\ell\)One branch's parameter perturbationsHow does that branch respond to learning?

The NTK uses the second matrix. It need not have a good spectrum merely because the first is isometric. It also depends on parameter coordinates and learning-rate multipliers.

Likewise, μP keeps feature updates at the right width scale, while CompleteP preserves certain nonlinear interactions inside deep blocks. Neither criterion alone forces all input-output Jacobian singular values to approach one.

For normalized layers, some directions are redundant and deliberately projected out. The LayerNorm calculation shows why it can be more meaningful to inspect singular values on the transmitted tangent subspace.

16. A small experiment with matching entry variances

  1. Choose width \(n=128\) and depths \(1,4,16,64\). Form deep linear products using iid Gaussian weights with variance \(1/n\), and separately using independent Haar orthogonal weights.
  2. Compute singular values of the actual product. Record \(m_1\), \(m_2\), singular-value quantiles, the minimum, and the maximum. Repeat over seeds.
  3. Compare the Gaussian moment trend with §8 and the orthogonal singular values with one. Finite-width deviations from the limiting Gaussian table are expected.
  4. Add ReLU after each layer, use the corresponding \(\sqrt2\) weight gain, and compute the input-dependent Jacobian at a fixed nonzero input. Inspect the zero singular values as well as the nonzero ones.

Use float64 for this small diagnostic. Long products can become numerically ill-conditioned; underflowed singular values are not automatically exact theoretical zeros. For much deeper products, use methods designed for stable spectral or Lyapunov-exponent estimation.

This is a reproducible experiment specification. The table in §8 is theoretical, and no empirical histogram is being claimed here. The elementary counterexample and the orthogonal-product identity can be checked without random sampling at all.

17. What to measure in a real network

A useful diagnostic records more than a single norm:

  • Average squared gain, to compare with variance propagation.
  • Spectral spread and extremes, to detect directions that the average hides.
  • Exact and numerical rank, interpreted relative to bottlenecks and normalization symmetries.
  • Dependence on input and training time, because nonlinear gates and learned weights change the Jacobian.
  • Alignment with task-relevant gradients or perturbations, because an isotropic average may not represent the task.

For a large model, Jacobian-vector and vector-Jacobian products can estimate gains or leading singular values without constructing the full Jacobian. A few random probes estimate an average; they do not certify the smallest singular value.

Good conditioning is one ingredient in useful learning. It does not supply a target-aligned representation, a suitable optimizer, or a guarantee that the property persists through training. Its value here is to expose failures invisible to the scalar initialization and update-scale calculations.

18. Five equations to remember

\[\boxed{J=U\operatorname{diag}(s_i)V^\top}\] \[\boxed{\mathbb E_v\|Jv\|^2=\frac1n\sum_i s_i^2\quad\text{for isotropic unit }v}\] \[\boxed{\|J^\top J-I\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad 1-\delta\leq s_i^2\leq1+\delta}\] \[\boxed{m_1=1,\quad m_2=L+1\quad\text{for the large-width Gaussian linear product}}\] \[\boxed{J_{\mathrm{plain}}=\prod_\ell D_\ell W_\ell,\qquad J_{\mathrm{res}}=\prod_\ell(I+a_\ell B_\ell)}\]

Variance preservation controls the mean squared gain. Isometry controls how that gain is distributed over directions. Orthogonality solves the linear problem exactly; nonlinear gates and residual dynamics determine how much of that solution survives in a network.
